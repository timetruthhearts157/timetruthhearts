Don't Dream It's Over

There is freedom within, there is freedom without

Try to catch the deluge in a paper cup

There's a battle ahead, many battles are lost

But you'll never see the end of the road while you're traveling with me

- Crowded House, Don't Dream It's Over

Life is beautiful and devastating.

The sight of the comet's debris so close, blazing a path across the night sky, was the most ethereally beautiful thing I had ever seen.

The fact that it would destroy Earth by morning was a devastating blow that I hadn't quite reconciled just yet.

The comet had approached directly from the Sun's direction, the glare obscuring it until it had passed out of it. It was too late then, ripped into pieces by the star's gravity and sent hurtling toward the planet. They would rain down on the Earth at a scale not even seen the day the dinosaurs were wiped from the planet. Nothing would survive as the cradle of Humanity was destroyed. It would be the end in an instant.

Disaster movies like to portray these scenarios with images of mass hysteria. panic, and pandemonium. But as I wandered the streets trying to figure out what to do, everything was eeriely quiet. It was a surprising twist that when all the world is going to end, people chose to use those last moments in love and connection, not panic and destruction. There was no need to panic and hoard, no one was surviving this, not even the billionaires hold up in their private bunkers in New Zealand.

I came to the street corner and stopped; at a literal and figurative crossroads. Go left and return to my current life, go right and strike out into the unknown.

I'd met her in a store a couple of months ago, bowling her over as she quietly purused the wine aisle. I was in a rush as usual, spurred by the voice in my head constantly reminding that I was taking too much time, screwing up, and that even though I had a list I was somehow hollow or deficient for following it. And also that I shouldn't deviate from it because otherwise why have a list? That voice was this strange, almost synthetic, bitonal mixture of my mother and my current girlfriend. What a weird fucking voice for an inner critic.

I was head down, rushing forward, and my face stuck in my phone; frantically checking my basket and checking off my list. I registered her feet just a fraction before I ran headlong into her body. Cute pointy toed leather boots was my first impression of her. Later, when we talked, she'd said she'd just put a bottle of wine back on the shelf. Otherwise the resulting impact would have knocked her down skidding across the floor, thrown my groceries every where, and smashed an expensive bottle of 10 year old Port for all of the trouble.

It's funny how life works isn't it?

NASA's SOHO satellite saw it first as it rounded perihelion and started to fracture and break. Later estimates would put it's size around 20 KM in diameter, an interstellar giant on a looping trajectory into the Solar System. Earth had five days before the fragments hit and vaporized everything. There was no hope, there was no salvation, no Hail Mary project we could throw against it. By the time the politicians and scientists agreed the best thing to do was tell the world the truth, Humanity had three days to make it's peace.

And with a night left before the end, I found myself on this street corner. Where would I go to watch the end? Who would I choose to spend this final night on Earth for all of us with?

That day when I met her, when I knocked her flat on her ass and skidding across the grocery store floor, I didn't think I would ever find myself here.

But there was something oddly freeing and focusing when you come face to face with the end. All the excuses, all the equivocations, all the things you tell yourself can wait until tomorrow evaporate away like a pot boiling dry. You're forced to stand there over the stove of your life and decide whether letting the pot burn and crack into ruin is finally worth it. Maybe it would just be easier to lift it off the burner and save what's left.

After the initial embarassment and stumbling, anxious apologies; I helped her get up off the floor. She was mad at first, and rightfully so. I'd been so wrapped up in my world I hadn't seen her. I was so wrapped up in my world for so long I hadn't seen a lot of things and hurt a lot of people in the process. Was she just the latest in a long line of people knocked flat and bowled over by my shortsightedness?

Eventually though, once I helped her pick everything up, we kept talking and her anger subsided. Which was good because I was struck by her simple beauty and fiery heart. Over the next month, I seemed to run into her more, figuratively thankfully, and we quickly formed a friendship. Isn't it weird how, sometimes, the worst circumstances can cause the best things to bloom?

We were sitting at a coffee shop one afternoon, when we both should have been working, having a . . . date? . . . when she finally addressed the question that had hung like a ghost lingering behind every one of our . . . dates? She brushed her hand against her dark skin, flipped the stray curls of her jet black, curly hair back over her shoulder, and dove straight in.

"What does your girlfriend think of all of this?" she asked with a knowing smile.

That day was two weeks before the Prime Minister's fateful, hastily-called news conference that was synchronized with every other world leader that cared to address their nation. In that moment, I thought I had the rest of my life to figure out what to do next. The rest of my life to make all the moves necessary to get from there to here. Little did I know that two weeks later I would be standing on a street corner trying to figure out how any of it made sense. And how little of it actually did.

I stammered for a moment, trying to make some space for a sensible reason or excuse. But as I thought about it, I knew there were a 1,000 little things that gave it away. The way I always insisted in daytime meet ups in coffee shops. The way I never asked her over to my place. The way I was evasive when she asked about my life with genuine interest. It didn't take clairvoyance, or even the barest of intuitions, to know that I was hiding something. Someone else in my life seemed to be the logical choice.

I sighed, dropped my shoulders, and looked down at the floor, "She doesn't. I think we both know that."

The truth was that we had drifted apart a long time ago, a cohabitation bourne mostly out of habit than love or desire any more. What was that line from that old Huey Lewis song? We thought about breaking up, but now we know it's much too late. We are bound by all the rest, like the same phone number, all the same friends, and the same address.

She smiled again, reached across the table, and took my hand.
